I want to actually make things this year. I want to be creative. But I want to be creative in a way that "builds community."
- I specifically saw videos about open source 3D printed CAD designs and custom PCBs that were used in them, and I thought that if I were to learn those tools, then I would be able to make some really cool things in the physical world that make a lot of people happy.

One way that I want to do this is by building physical things with my hands that I'm able to share with a community of people locally. It doesn't *have* to be local, but there is a certain "human" aspect about building things in and for a specific community that you're a part of. 

I think that working in this way is a good example of working in a community that leads to a *deeply* positive outcome. I emphasize *deeply* because I think that there is something more to this way of working in a community than other contemporary examples, and I want to discuss those contemporary examples to see what they're missing.

## The pull of the vicious community

%% This section could be shortened. I don't need to tell everyone this story in so much detail. %%

Close to a decade ago now, I would spend many of my evenings joining a voice call to play video games with a few other people. I only knew two out of approximately six of the people in this call IRL, and I had only met one of those two in-person in the past year. The extent of this community was the following:

- Play an average of 2 hours of (usually) competitive video games each night.
- Broadcasting reposts of memes to each other in a text chat.

That's it. Looking back at this, I would deem this community to be "low quality." That's not to say that these people were bad people – it's just that we weren't experiencing a very strong mutual benefit by being around each other. The main purpose of this group was a place for us to consume content without feeling as lonely as we may have felt outside of the call. And, crucially, that content was not benefiting us, either. 

At a certain point, I realized that this community wasn't making me a better person and, in many ways, it was making me a *worse* person. The games we played would either overstimulate me when I played well, or made me frustrated and angry when I played poorly. I was pulled into joining these calls on a regular basis because, well, what else was I going to do? I didn't have any other hobbies, and I was stressed and tired from college work. I thought that hanging out with these folks was a good way for me to unwind and relax.

The same thing is said by people who become regulars at a bar after long days at work, where the vice of alcohol is present. And the same thing is said by the junkie addicted to heroin who doesn't have non-junkie friends when they're looking for someone to hang out with[^1]. And then they end up shooting up again because why wouldn't you do that if that's what your community is?

The thing in common with these communities is that they center around a *vice*, whether it be alcohol, narcotics, or even something as benign as video games. It is not wrong to play games to relax, but a community that encourages you to compulsively indulge in entertainment – physically addictive or not – is one that I would call vicious. 

People need communities that instill *virtues* in them.

## The path to the virtuous community

I eventually stopped joining these calls and stopped talking with this group of people entirely. I felt as though something was missing in my life, and I needed to force myself to spend time thinking about how I preferred to spend my time. This ultimatum eventually lead to other intellectual and spiritual practices of mine – reading, writing (blog posts and personal journals), meditation, etc. I found how to make myself a better person while I'm alone. But I wanted a group with which I could have deep dialectics with on these topics. 

I moved into the city, hoping that the larger social pool would lead to me running into like-minded individuals. But I didn't find the community I was looking for – I just found a larger pool of people who wanted to remain insular. Eventually, life events pulled me back towards my family and I currently live in a small town

## The human element that contemporary community misses

%% Outline %% 

- Experiencing human connection beyond just a computer screen is good. Patting someone on the back, seeing their face in all three dimensions and all lighting, at their worst and at their best, rain or shine.
	- The computer enables *more* connection, but it does not necessarily enable high quality connection. Technology's march is incessant, and the level of connection one is able to experience in our time is far better than it was a decade ago, let alone a century ago, but it is still not the same as being in the same physical location as someone.
- Communication mediums in a community are vast and one must strike a balance between the effective and the personal.
	- One on one in person chats are great. DMs via texting are quick and efficient. A community that is striving to *do something* needs to find the right balance between these two things. 
		- That is, you cannot have a deep community simply via tweeting and all using the same hashtags or being close to each other in users' following graph. That is a para-social community. 
			- Micro-blogging "communities" are not communities – they are people using the least personal form of communication with one another while being incentivized for "engagement" which inevitably leads to negative outcomes.

# Footnotes

[^1] I do not equate video games to heroin. One is clearly far more addictive than the other (and will much more easily kill you). My point is that the communities you engage with shape you and, for better or worse, tend to try and pull you back in to them.
